Where does it live?

Deployment and sovereignty: this classroom tool, and a real platform deployment

All sessions Investigate Build Oversight Glossary

Every other page of this tool asks who may see what. This one asks where the software and the data physically are, who keeps them up to date, and who could read them. Switch between the two views and compare.

Teaching extension The classroom view is exact. The real view is our drawing from Palantir's public descriptions and public reporting; Palantir publishes no deployment diagram of any customer.

This classroom tool

Where the parts live

  • Code and synthetic data: static files on GitHub Pages (both seed.js files are generated from the CSVs in synthetic_data/).
  • Map tiles: OpenStreetMap's tile servers, only for the Map tab.
  • Session, audit log, governance: your browser's localStorage, under dc-palantir:*.
  • Server or database: none.

Who could read what

  • You, or anyone at your laptop: everything, including what your role "cannot see".
  • GitHub: which files were requested, and from where.
  • OpenStreetMap: which map squares were viewed, and from where.
  • The instructor, the university, Palantir: nothing, unless you show them.

A real deployment

Where the parts live

  • The platform (Gotham, Foundry, AIP) runs in the customer's environments: public cloud, on-premises servers, classified networks, edge devices.
  • The data comes from the customer's own systems through connectors, and Actions write back to them.
  • The software is kept up to date by Apollo, Palantir's continuous delivery system, across the whole fleet.
  • The pipelines are often written by Palantir's forward-deployed engineers working inside the customer.

Who could read what

  • Customer users: what their role, markings and Checkpoints allow.
  • Customer administrators: they decide the roles; who checks them?
  • Palantir staff: whatever the contract and the deployment permit; that is a legal question, not a technical one.
  • Auditors and the public: only on request. The Dutch police platform became known through freedom-of-information requests, with about 99% of the documents redacted.

The honest answer: this classroom tool is more sovereign than any real deployment: nothing leaves your laptop except requests for files and map tiles, and nobody outside it can read your log. It is also less safe: there is no real access control, and anything a role "cannot see" is one click away in the developer tools.

Denmark: POL-INTEL and European exits

Danish police use POL-INTEL, a customised version of Gotham, studied by Galis and Karlsson (2025) under the title "Scientification through privatization". A separate matter is defence: Euronews (June 2026) reports that Denmark will look for local replacements for a seven-year Palantir deal for Maven Smart System and Foundry, and that the Dutch State Secretary for Defence wants a "fully fledged alternative" within two years. That report does not mention POL-INTEL.

  1. What is sovereignty when the platform, and its updates, are delivered by the vendor?
  2. Which decisions in a pipeline are technical, and which are policy made by whoever writes the transforms?

Sources: researcher.itu.dk, euronews.com
