# Who decides, and who gets to see that they decided? A run through the Palantir-style platform

*Joris, BSc Computer Science, elective in critical data studies*

## What I did

I worked through all three sessions of the runthrough in a fresh browser profile with localStorage cleared. I also did what the guide doesn't ask for: I switched roles in the middle of flows, used two tabs, typed odd inputs, read localStorage, and downloaded the JavaScript and seed files that any visitor gets. Map tiles are blocked in my sandbox, so the map is grey; that is my environment, not the tool. The full list of problems is in `issues.md`.

## What the tool taught me

**Ontology and markings.** The first exercise works because it is so simple. As Patrol officer, "Trace the risk-tagged operator" scores 14 and shows "0 accounts, $0 linked transfers". As Financial-crime analyst, the same scenario scores 22 with four accounts and $23,460, and the phone data turns into hatched "withheld — marking TELECOM" cells instead. The header keeps a running count ("27 objects and 108 links withheld from your role"), and a line under each gap answers "Why can't I see this?" by naming the roles that could. The tool made me see each role's view as a picture of its own, not a filter over one true picture. The Leads tab pushes this furthest. The care worker Omar Rahman is the top enforcement lead for the data protection officer (85.8, mostly from a clinic registration), seventh for Patrol (39.9, from phone pings) and has no address at all for the analyst. The formula is printed in full, which I liked very much.

**Resolution.** The Resolution tab in Build is the best part of the tool. At 0.75 every candidate pair stays separate. At 0.60, M. Singh and Maya Singh merge, which is correct. At 0.55 the watchlisted Omar Al-Rahman merges with the care worker. The explanation adds it up: same name +0.45, Omar/Umar +0.15, conflicting dates of birth −0.15, shared LHR–IST route +0.10. Afterwards the DPO sees a "dual-use broker" living at 41 Oak Lane, Birmingham. The same dial both fixes real duplicates and creates a false one, and the arithmetic sits there for anyone to argue with. I then ran *Unmerge person* as analyst, and the pair shows "rejected by override" with my reason quoted. Only one thing confused me: once merged, the person's resolution record still said "Matched by passport · confidence 1.00", which is exactly the false certainty the tab warns about.

**Actions and writeback.** The Action drawer shows everything in order before you commit: parameters, role check, Checkpoint, purpose rules, a before/after table, and the exact message for "Border watch". I put Daniel Wu on the watchlist, and the Watchlist operators rule picked him up at once, with the footnote "The rule cannot tell an Action's tag from a source tag: the analysis feeds on what it produced." That line explains feedback loops better than any reading I've done. *Remove from watchlist* on Kareem Haddad is refused because his tag comes from the source data,, with a clear explanation.

**Checkpoints and the agent.** "routine" gets you through an expansion. With Strict mode, so does forty times the letter "a". The tool presents this as the lesson, and I agree: a prompt records an intention, it doesn't check it. The simulated agent is honest about what it is. Its trace header says "mode scripted · failure mode fabricated link". Asked what links Omar Rahman to North Quay Logistics, it confidently cites `beneficially_owns::person-g5510077-company-co-102`, which does not exist. Evals only flags this after I press Check. After *Approve all*, Evals says "No edit was applied without approval" and then adds "approved, but did anyone read them?"

## What I found when I probed

The tool says openly that it is client-side. The deployment page states that the developer tools "show every seed row and every log entry, whatever the role: the roles model access control; they do not enforce it", and that is true. So I didn't count "I can read the seed" against it. What interested me more was whether the simulation keeps to **its own rules**, because that is what students will base their conclusions on. Mostly it does, but not everywhere.

First, the **Objects editor gets around the whole governance layer**. As analyst I typed `sanctions-evasion` into Ben Grant's risk-tag field, and he went straight onto the Watchlist operators list, with the footnote saying "0 objects risk-tagged by Actions". I then deleted Kareem's source watchlist record, WL-1001, which the Action had just refused to touch. There was no confirmation, no Checkpoint, and no audit event, only two `object.view` lines. *Reset dataset* isn't logged either. The guide says every Action leaves a trail, and on the Actions surface it does. But it took me two minutes to find a back door that leaves none. Admin tools being where governance leaks out is a real lesson about platforms, but I don't think the tool means to teach it.

Second, **Export JSON leaks**. The entities in my analyst export were redacted properly, but the `leads` block held all ten clinic home addresses, with purpose "healthcare" and coordinates, and eighteen TELECOM ping addresses. The Checkpoint had told me I was exporting what was "visible to Financial-crime analyst".

Third, **approving an agent's edit skips the Checkpoint**. When I ran *Add to watchlist* by hand, I had to state a purpose. *Approve*, or *Approve all*, on the agent's staged edits went through with no prompt and sent watchlist messages to Border watch. While doing this I put the care worker on the watchlist by mistake: "Summarise Omar Rahman" quietly stages that edit, and I clicked Approve to see what would happen. I undid it, but how easily it happened makes the Maven point better than the Maven card does. I can't tell whether that is intended.

Smaller points. The phone-search Checkpoint only fires at seven or more digits, so the guide's `+357` does nothing, and as Patrol `118890` found Kareem without any prompt. Object IDs contain phone numbers: C. Silva's withheld number +30131994523 is the end of `person::c-silva-30131994523`, which the analyst can read. The expansion Checkpoint lists the location IDs (Seabright Ave, Marina Free Zone) before you have justified anything. And when the DPO removes HEALTH from the clinic file, the Platform contractor can suddenly read patient names and dates of birth, because that file carried no PII marking underneath.

Finally, the **audit chain**. Changing one justification is detected ("broken at ev-000019"). Deleting the last 120 events is not detected. Deleting the first 80 and resetting the anchor hash produces "verified · 80 older events dropped (cap 5000)", an invented explanation. The Auditor, who is not an instructor, can clear the whole log with the reason "x", and if I then write my backup back, even the clear disappears. A comment in the source file admits that "anyone with devtools can rewrite the whole chain". On screen, though, the green "verified" badge says more than it should.

There were also things I tried and could not break. With a DPO marking-removal Checkpoint open in one tab, I switched to Platform contractor in a second tab and pressed Continue in the first. The removal was refused and logged under the contractor. Ten spaces don't count as a reason.

## Where it convinces and where it is theatre

It convinces where the mechanism *is* the argument. Resolution weights, the lead formula, markings flowing down the lineage DAG, the retention clock showing 18 leads still built on expired pings: all of these are real computations I could check, and they behave as claimed.

It is theatre in places, and mostly it admits it. The agent is a script. Karlsruhe mode is "our abstraction of the ruling's logic, not its text". The guide also asks for things I couldn't produce. I never got a "Blocked by rule R2" card in Analytics, for any role or purpose. The Leads step for R2 can't be observed: only the analyst can choose *Financial crime*, and the analyst can't see HEALTH anyway. The patrol/analyst behaviour in 3.3 was the reverse of what the guide describes. And as analyst I simply had no *Expand devices* button in 1.3. A less stubborn student would assume they had made a mistake.

## Does it matter pedagogically?

The client-side limits don't matter much, because the tool says so and the deployment page turns that into a lesson. The inconsistencies inside the simulation do matter. If an Action is governed but the same edit made in the schema editor isn't, a student draws one of two conclusions: either "Actions are theatre" or "governance is complete". Neither is what the tool wants to teach. The same goes for the export leak and the Checkpoint that agent approvals skip. They are actually *good* material: back doors, exports and automation shortcuts are how this goes wrong in real deployments. But they need to be either fixed or made visible and named, the way the tool names everything else.

## Overall

I came in expecting a slick demo and leave with a better grasp of entity resolution and purpose limitation than two lectures gave me. The resolution exercise and the leads formula are excellent, and the honesty chips earn their place. The tool is less consistent than it claims: about five things undercut its own rules, and the runthrough has drifted away from the build in several steps. I would recommend it. I would also set the probing as an assignment, because finding the back door taught me more than the door did.
